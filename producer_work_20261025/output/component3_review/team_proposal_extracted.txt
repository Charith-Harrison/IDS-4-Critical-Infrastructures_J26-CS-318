Protocol-aware ML framework for intelligent Modbus attack detection and incident analysis 
AIMS - Autonomous Intelligent Machines and Systems  
 
Cyber Security (CS) 
 
Nature and Gravity of the Problem  
Industrial Control Systems (ICS) and Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) environments 
for the backbone of modern critical infrastructure such as power generation, water treatment, 
manufacturing and oil gas pipelines [1], [4]. Large proportions of these systems communicate using 
Modbus/TCP protocol, which was designed decades ago for reliability and simplicity rather than for 
security [8], [9]. Modbus contains no native authentication, no encryption and no integrity checking, 
which means that any device able to reach the network can read, process data, issue control 
commands or impersonate legitimate masters and slaves [11], [13].  
As ICS networks are increasingly connected to corporate networks and the internet to enable remote 
monitoring, the attack surface has expanded dramatically [4], [1 1]. Adversaries can now launch 
Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks that flood Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs) with malformed or 
high-frequency requests, reconnaissance scans that quietly map registers and devices, command and 
response injection that forges process commands, and replay attacks that re -transmit previously 
captured legitimate packets [13]. Conventional Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) deployed in these 
networks rely mainly on signature-based or static rule-based methods and on generic network-flow 
features that ignore the semantics of the Modbus protocol [1,], [6], [15]. As a result they fail to detect 
novel, low-volume or protocol -valid attacks, and produce high false -positive rates that overwhelm 
operators [10], [15].  
This research therefore investigates a unified, protocol -aware machine learning framework that 
detects and explains four distinct classes of Modbus attack; DoS, reconnaissance, 
command/response injection and replay. Each through a methodology tailored to the behavioral

6. Description of Existing Research and Systems (300 words max) 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Summary of existing products/systems 
Most operational deployments protect Modbus networks using perimeter firewalls, industrial 
demilitarized zones and signature-based intrusion detection sensors such as Snort with hand-written 
ICS rule sets [15]. Commercial OT-security platforms provide deep-packet inspection and asset 
discovery for Modbus and similar protocols, but they are expensive, largely rule-driven and tuned to 
known attack signatures [15]. Research testbeds such as the Mississippi State University gas-pipeline 
and water-storage datasets [2], [8], recent Modbus/TCO attack-emulation testbeds [13], [18] have 
been used to generate labelled normal and attack traffic covering reconnaissance, injection, DoS and 
replay scenarios.  
Summary of existing research 
Machine learning and deep learning have become dominant research direction for ICS intrusion 
detection [1], [4]. Supervised classifiers such as Decision Trees, Random Forest, SVM, KNN and 
XGBoost have been applied to Modbus traffic, with Random Forest and gradient boosting models 
reaching very high reported accuracy on benchmark datasets [5], [6]. Deep models including DNN, 
CNN and hybrid GooLeNet-LSTM architecture have been used to capture spatial and temporal patterns 
in ICS communication for mutli-class attack detection [3]. Unsupervised approaches particularly 
autoencoders that learn a model of normal traffic and flag high reconstruction error have proven 
effective for anomaly and zero-day detection in SCADA, DNP3 networks, including function-code based 
autoencoder IDS designs [9], [13]. Protocol-specific work has demonstrated replay-attack detection 
using timestamps and sequence numbers [10] and explainable-AI methods that take Modbus IDS 
decisions interpretable [6], [7]. 
Gaps/limitations identified 
• Most IDS use generic flow features (packet size, bytes/sec, TCP flags) and ignore Modbus 
protocol semantics such as function codes and register access patterns [1], [6]. 
• Signature and rule-based system cannot detect novel, low-volume or protocol-valid attacks 
and generate high false-positive rates [15]. 
• Reconnaissance scans rarely change traffic volume, so packet-level detectors miss the subtle 
temporal behavior that distinguishes them [3], [18]. 
• Injection attacks are frequently inspected per-packet, ignoring whether a command is valid 
within its surrounding communication context [9], [13]. 
• Replay attacks reuse genuinely valid packets, so content-based detection alone fails without 
transaction-consistency analysis [10]. 
• Few works integrate detection of multiple attack classes into one framework with unified 
incident analysis and explanation [4], [7]. 
Hence there is a clear need for a single, cost-effective, protocol-aware machine-learning framework 
that combines attack-specific detection methodologies with unified incident analysis for Modbus-
based industrial control systems.

7. Brief description of the solution's nature, including a conceptual diagram (maximum 500 
words). 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Addressing the identified research gaps 
The proposed solution is a Protocol-aware machine learning framework that captures live and 
recorded Modbus/TCP traffic, parses it into a shared, protocol-aware repository, and then routes 
those features to four parallel detection modules, one per attack class, before fusing their outputs into 
a single incident-analysis and reporting layer. Rather than treating every attack with one generic 
model, each research components apply the methodology best matched to the behavioral signature of 
its target attack.  
• RP1 – DoS via Protocol-aware feature engineering: engineers Modbus-specific features 
(function codes, register/coil addresses, transaction IDs, read/write behaviour) and uses an 
autoencoder to flag flooding and resource-exhaustion anomalies that generic flow feature 
miss.  
• RP2 – Reconnaissance via temporal behavior analysis: models communication patterns over 
time using time-series/LSTM techniques to reveal sequential register scanning and abnormal 
polling frequency that produce no obvious volume spike.  
• RP3 – Command/response injection via context-aware command analysis: analyses 
command relationships and protocol semantics. For example, an unexpected write after read, 
invalid function-code usage, or abnormal request-response pairing instead of inspecting 
packet in isolation.  
• RP4 – Replay via transaction consistency analysis: examines transaction IDs, response timing, 
duplicate requests and session consistency to separate replayed but valid packets from 
genuine communication.  
A common feature repository removes duplicated preprocessing, lets the four modules share a 
consistent view of the traffic, and supports cross-module correlation. A decision-fusion layer combines 
per-module anomaly scores into a unified verdict, and an incident-analysis layer adds attack-type 
classification, severity ranking and explainable-AI attribution so that operators understand why an 
alert was raised. Because the framework relies on open datasets, an inexpensive testbed and software 
models rather than proprietary appliances, it offers an affordable and adaptable alternative for 
securing Modbus ICS networks.

8. Brief overview of the availability of necessary specialized domain expertise, knowledge, 
and data. (500 words max) 
 
 
 
Gaining specialized domain expertise  
The team will build domain expertise in industrial control systems and operational-technology 
security by consulting academic supervisors, cyber-security practitioners and where possible, OT/ICS 
engineers who operate Modbus-based equipment. The group will study how Modbus/TCP 
communication works in practice, the master-slave request/response model, function codes, register 
and coil addressing and the MBAP header and how each target attack manifests at the protocol level 
[8], [11], [13]. Members will review the structure and behavior of SCADA/HMI systems, PLCs and 
field devices so that modelling of normal versus malicious behavior is grounded in realistic 
operational patterns. Regular discussion with supervisors and review of standards and case studies 
on ICS incidents will deepen the team’s understanding of attacker techniques, defensive controls and 
the safety implications of false negatives and false positives in critical infrastructure.  
Gaining technical knowledge and skills 
The technical skills required for the project span network traffic analysis, protocol parsing, feature 
engineering and machine/deep learning. Team members will acquire these through online courses, 
academic literature, technical documentation and hands-on experiment. Specific competencies 
include packet capture and dissection (e.g- with Wireshark/tshark and Scapy), building and labelling 
datasets, and engineering protocol-aware features from Modbus traffic. On the modelling side, 
members will learn to design and evaluate autoencoders for anomaly detection, LSTM and other 
time-series models for temporal behavior, sequence and context modelling techniques for command 
analysis, and consistency-checking methods for transaction analysis [3], [9], [12]. The team will also 
develop skills in building the integration layer and incident dashboard, including data visualization, 
model service and explainable AI techniques for interpreting detections [6], [7]. Standard evaluation 
methodology with accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, false positive rate and ROC-AUC will be used 
to compare models against baselines [5], [6], [10]. 
Data collection 
The project will use both primary and secondary data. Primary data will be generate from a 
controlled Modbus/TCP testbed using physical or simulated PLCs and a Modbus master, supported 
by simulation frameworks such as ICSSIM and Modbus attack-emulation tools in which the four 
target attacks (DoS, reconnaissance, command/response injection and replay) are executed to 
capture labelled normal and malicious traffic [13], [18]. Secondary data will be drawn from 
established public ICS/SCADA datasets, including the Mississippi State University gas-pipeline and 
water-storage datasets and recent Modbus-specific anomaly detection datasets, to benchmark the 
models and improve generalization [2], [8], [17]. Collected traffic will be parsed, cleaned, labelled 
and split into training and testing sets within the common feature repository, and will be the basis 
for training and validating every detection module.

Ethical clearance 
Because the research uses network traffic from controlled testbeds and publicly available datasets rather 
than personal or medical data, the ethical risk is low. No human subject data is collected. All traffic is 
generated in an isolated laboratory environment so that no live or third-party infrastructure is affected. 
Any third-party datasets will be used strictly in accordance with their licenses and only for academic 
research. The project will follow responsible disclosure principles for any vulnerabilities observed and will 
adhere to the university’s research ethics guidelines, ensuring that the work is conducted safely, legally 
and for defensive purposes only.
