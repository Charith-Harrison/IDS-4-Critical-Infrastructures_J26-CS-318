ABSTRACT 
Industrial Control Systems (ICS) and Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) 
environments underpin critical infrastructure such as power generation, water treatment and 
manufacturing, and a large proportion of them communicate using the Modbus/TCP  protocol 
[1], [4]. Because Modbus was designed decades ago for reliability rather than security, it 
provides no native authentication, encryption or integrity checking, so any device that can reach 
the network can read data, issue control commands or impersonate a legitimate master or slave 
[8], [9], [11]. As these networks become increasingly connected to corporate IT and the internet, 
adversaries can launch Denial -of-Service attacks, reconnaissance scans, command/response 
injection and replay attacks, wh ile conventional signature -based intrusion detection systems 
(IDS) rely on generic network -flow features that ignore Modbus protocol semantics and 
therefore miss novel or protocol-valid attacks [1], [6], [15]. 
This individual component addresses reconnaissance: attackers sequentially scan Modbus 
registers and devices to map a network before a targeted attack, yet this scanning rarely changes 
overall traffic volume, so packet -level and volume -based detectors do n ot notice it [3], [18]. 
The literature confirms that reconnaissance is distinguished by subtle temporal behavior rather 
than packet content, yet few Modbus IDS studies explicitly model this temporal dimension 
within a unified multi-attack framework - the specific gap this research addresses. 
The proposed contribution is a temporal  behavior-analysis component that builds time -
windowed, sequential features from Modbus/TCP communication - register/coil access order, 
polling-rate drift and inter -request timing - and trains a Long Short -Term Memory (LSTM) 
model to classify normal versus reconnaissance behavior. The component will be developed 
using a controlled Modbus/TCP testbed (ICSSIM) supplemented with public ICS/SCADA 
datasets, following a quantitative, exper imental methodology, and an iterative, experiment -
driven development approach. Performance will be benchmarked against packet-level and non-
temporal baselines using precision, recall, F1 -score, false -positive rate, ROC -AUC and 
detection latency, validated through session -independent evaluation to avoid data leakage. 
Within the overall protocol -aware detection framework, this component supplies a 
reconnaissance classification, confidence score and explanation to the shared decision -fusion 
and incident-analysis layer, contributing to a unified, cost-effective Modbus intrusion detection 
and incident-analysis solution for industrial control systems. 
Keywords: Modbus/TCP, reconnaissance detection, temporal behavior analysis, LSTM, 
intrusion detection, industrial control systems.

1. INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Background and Context 
Industrial Control Systems (ICS) and Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) 
environments form the backbone of modern critical infrastructure, including power generation, water 
treatment, manufacturing and oil and gas pipelines [1], [4]. A large p roportion of these systems 
communicate using the Modbus/TCP protocol, which was designed decades ago for reliability and 
simplicity rather than for security [8], [9]. Modbus has no native authentication, no encryption and no 
integrity checking, so any devi ce able to reach the network can read process data, issue control 
commands, or impersonate a legitimate master or slave [11], [13]. 
As ICS networks are increasingly connected to corporate networks and the internet to enable remote 
monitoring, the attack surface has expanded dramatically [4], [11]. Adversaries can now launch Denial-
of-Service (DoS) attacks that flood Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs) with malformed or high -
frequency requests, reconnaissance scans that quietly map registers and devices, command and response 
injection that forges process commands, and replay attacks that re -transmit previously captured 
legitimate packets [13]. Conventional intrusion detection systems (IDS) deployed in these networks rely 
mainly on signature-based or static rule-based methods and on generic network-flow features that ignore 
the semantics of the Modbus protocol [1], [6], [15]. As a result, they fail to detect novel, low-volume or 
protocol-valid attacks, and they produce high false-positive rates that overwhelm operators [10], [15]. 
The wider group project therefore investigates a unified, protocol -aware machine learning framework 
that detects and explains four distinct classes of Modbus attack  - DoS, reconnaissance, 
command/response injection and replay - each through a methodology tailored to the behavioral 
signature of that attack, integrated into a single intelligent detection and incident-analysis pipeline. The 
impact of leaving this problem unaddressed is significant across several dimensions. Operati onally, 
undetected attacks on PLCs and field devices can cause incorrect actuator behavior, equipment damage, 
process disruption and even physical safety hazards for personnel and the public [4], [13]. Technically, 
existing IDS cannot model protocol semant ics or temporal behavior and degrade against zero -day or 
protocol-valid attacks [1], [6], [15]. Economically, downtime in critical infrastructure, recovery costs 
and regulatory penalties are extremely high, while specialized commercial OT -security appliances are 
costly and difficult to adapt [4], [11]. From a privacy and trust perspective, manipulation or exfiltration 
of process data undermines the integrity and trustworthiness of national infrastructure and the data it 
generates [7], [11]. 
Most operational deployments currently protect Modbus networks with perimeter firewalls, industrial 
demilitarized zones and signature-based sensors such as Snort with hand-written ICS rule sets, or with 
commercial OT-security platforms that provide deep-packet inspection but are expensive, largely rule-
driven and tuned to known signatures [15]. Research testbeds such as the Mississippi State University 
gas-pipeline and water-storage datasets [2], [8] and recent Modbus attack-emulation testbeds [13], [18] 
have been used to generate labelled normal and attack traffic, and these resources are used across all 
four components of this project. The proposed group solution is a protocol -aware machine learning 
framework that captures live and recorded Modbus/TCP traffic, parses it into a shared, protocol-aware 
feature repository, and routes those features to four parallel detection modules - one per attack class - 
before fusing their outputs into a single incident-analysis and reporting layer, rather than treating every 
attack with one generic model. 
This project aligns with the AIMS (Autonomous Intelligent Machines and Systems) research group and 
the Cyber Security specialization, within the Cyber Security and Network Engineering / Software

1.2 Literature Review 
This review is scoped to the individual research problem investigated in this component - reconnaissance 
detection - rather than the wider group problem: the sub-objective is to model Modbus communication 
patterns over time, rather than analyzing packets independently, because reconnaissance rarely changes 
traffic volume and is instead revealed through temporal behavior , sequential register access and 
abnormal polling frequency that volume-based detectors miss. 
Gao and Morris [15] provide a systematic evaluation of signature-based IDS for Modbus and show that 
packet-level signatures cannot detect protocol -valid or low -volume attacks such as reconnaissance 
scanning; this finding is the central piece of evidence mo tivating a temporal, behavior-based approach 
for this component rather than a further signature- or packet-based one. Chu, Lai and Liu [3] proposed 
a hybrid GoogLeNet -LSTM architecture for multi -classification ICS intrusion detection, combining 
spatial and temporal feature extraction on labelled ICS traffic; their method demonstrates that a recurrent 
(LSTM) component can capture sequential communication patterns, which directly supports the choice 
of an LSTM -based approach for this component. However, their evaluation does not isolate 
reconnaissance/scanning behavior as a distinct class, does not report session-independent generalization, 
and does not evaluate a dedicated reconnaissance feature set - limitations this research addresses directly. 
Attack-emulation testbeds such as those used by Bashendy  et al. [13] and in real -time cyber-physical 
testbed studies [18] demonstrate practical methods for generating and capturing labelled 
reconnaissance/scanning traffic against Modbus/TCP, and this component draws on the same class of 
testbed tooling (ICSSIM and Modbus attack -emulation utilities) for primary data collection. 
Explainable-AI methods applied to Modbus and ICS IDS decisions by Tawfik et al. [6] and Aslam et al. 
[7] make IDS output interpretable to operators; their findings directly influence the e xplainability 
requirement placed on this component's output - that a reconnaissance alert must expose the contributing 
behavioral features rather than a bare classification - even though neither study targets reconnaissance 
specifically. 
Existing products that attempt to address Modbus reconnaissance are largely signature- and perimeter-
based: commercial OT-security platforms provide deep-packet inspection and asset discovery, but are 
expensive, rule-driven and tuned to known attack signatures rather than to behavioral scanning patterns 
[15]. None of the reviewed products or systems provide a dedicated, temporal -behavior-based 
reconnaissance detector; this component addresses that limitation directly by modelling communication 
behavior over time rather than relying on per-packet signatures or volumetric thresholds.

1.3 Research Gap 
Reconnaissance scans rarely change overall traffic volume, so packet-level and volume-based detectors 
miss the subtle temporal behavior that distinguishes scanning from normal polling [3], [18]. Existing 
Modbus IDS studies either evaluate generic flow -based classifiers that are not designed to model 
ordering and timing [1], [6], [15], or apply temporal/recurrent models without isolating reconnaissance 
as a distinct class or testing session-independent generalization [3]. 
The specific research gap this component investigates is therefore: there is currently no validated, 
protocol-aware temporal-sequence model that reliably distinguishes Modbus reconnaissance scanning 
from normal polling behavior without relying on traffic -volume anomalies, and no such detector has 
been integrated into a unified, multi-attack Modbus incident-analysis pipeline.

2. OBJECTIVE 
2.1 Main Objective 
To design and validate an LSTM -based temporal behavior analysis approach for detecting Modbus 
reconnaissance and scanning activity that produces no traffic -volume anomaly, and to integrate this 
detection capability into the shared protocol -aware Modbus int rusion detection and incident -analysis 
framework. 
2.2 Specific Objectives 
• To capture and label reconnaissance/scanning traffic exhibiting sequential register scanning and 
polling-rate changes, using a controlled Modbus/TCP testbed. 
• To construct time -windowed and sequential features that represent Modbus communication 
behavior over time, including register/coil access order, polling-rate drift, request counts and inter-
request timing. 
• To design and train time -series/LSTM models capable of detecting subtle temporal scanning 
patterns from these windowed sequences. 
• To validate that session -independent temporal modelling outperforms packet -level and non -
temporal baseline detection. 
• To evaluate detection performance (precision, recall, F1-score, false-positive rate, ROC-AUC) and 
real-time detection latency against defined targets. 
• To integrate the reconnaissance detection module's output - classification, confidence score and 
explanation - into the shared decision-fusion and incident-analysis layer.

3. METHODOLOGY 
3.1 Research Methodology / Design 
A quantitative, experimental research design is appropriate for this component. The research question - 
whether temporal, protocol -aware sequence modelling can distinguish reconnaissance from normal 
Modbus polling more reliably than packet-level analysis - is a measurable classification problem rather 
than an exploratory or attitudinal one. The available data is structured, timestamped network traffic that 
can be windowed into fixed-length sequences, which suits a supervised time-series classification design. 
The experimental requirements - a controlled testbed, labelled attack and normal traffic, and a defined 
baseline for comparison - are all achievable within the shared group testbed. This design is consistent 
with related work such as Chu et al. [3], who evaluated a recurrent architecture experimentally against 
baseline classifiers on labelled ICS traffic. The selected methodology is  therefore a quantitative, 
experimental research design combining controlled data collection with comparative model evaluation. 
3.2 Data / Participants / Experimental Inputs 
No human participants are involved; the experimental inputs are Modbus/TCP network traffic 
sequences. Primary data will be generated from a controlled Modbus/TCP testbed using ICSSIM and 
Modbus attack-emulation tools, in which reconnaissance/scanning traffic (sequential register scanning, 
polling-rate changes) is executed alongside normal master -slave polling traffic and captured via port 
mirroring [13], [18]. As preliminary evidence of data availability, a first-pass primary dataset of 175,800 
windowed sequences (20 timesteps by 7 protocol -aware features per window, spanning 13 captured 
sessions) has already been assembled from an initial testbed run, confirming that the required data 
pipeline (capture → protocol parsing → windowing → labelling) is achievable. 
Secondary data will be drawn from established public ICS/SCADA datasets, including the Mississippi 
State University gas-pipeline and water-storage datasets [2], [8] and recent Modbus -specific anomaly 
detection datasets [17], to benchmark the models and imp rove generalization. Collected traffic will be 
parsed, cleaned, labelled and split into training and testing sets within the shared common feature 
repository. 
A specific data -quality issue identified during preliminary analysis is that positive (reconnaissance) 
labels in the first -pass dataset are concentrated in very few sessions, which risks session -specific 
overfitting rather than genuinely generalizable scan detection. The data-preparation phase will therefore 
explicitly prioritize capturing reconnaissance traffic across a wider and more balanced set of sessions, 
and all evaluation will be performed using a session-independent split (Section 3.5) to detect and report 
this risk honestly rather than mask it. 
3.3 Baseline / Benchmark 
The proposed temporal LSTM approach will be compared against two baselines: (1) a packet -level / 
static-feature detector representative of conventional signature - and flow-based IDS approaches [15], 
and (2) a non-temporal machine-learning baseline (Random Forest trained on the same protocol-aware 
features, flattened across the window) to isolate the specific benefit of modelling the temporal sequence 
rather than the benefit of protocol -aware features alone. This two -baseline design directly tests the 
novelty claim of this component: that temporal modelling, not just protocol awareness, is required to 
detect reconnaissance.

3.5 Validation Strategy 
Results will be validated using GroupKFold (leave -one-session-out) cross -validation, grouped by 
capture session, to prevent near -duplicate sliding-window sequences from the same session leaking 
across the train/test split - a recognized risk in windowed network -traffic datasets. To make this risk 
explicit rather than assumed, session -independent results will be reported alongside a naive random -
split result for the same data, demonstrating the size of any leakage effect. Thi s approach follows 
accepted evaluation practice in the ICS intrusion -detection literature, which emphasizes accuracy, 
precision, recall, F1 -score, false-positive rate and ROC -AUC as standard comparison metrics against 
baselines [5], [6], [10]. 
 
3.6 Development Approach 
An iterative, experiment -driven development approach (short cycles of feature engineering, model 
training, evaluation and refinement) is appropriate given the exploratory, research -heavy nature of 
model development and the need for the component to integra te continuously with the shared feature 
repository used by the other three detection modules. Each iteration will produce a working, evaluable 
version of the detector, allowing early and frequent validation against the metrics in Table 1 and early 
detection of integration issues with the shared repository and decision-fusion layer.

4. HIGH-LEVEL SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 
The proposed group solution is a seven -layer, protocol-aware machine learning framework (Table 5). 
Layer 1 is the Modbus/TCP ICS testbed environment, where normal and adversarial (DoS, 
reconnaissance, injection and replay) traffic is generated. Layer 2 acquires this traffic through live packet 
capture and public Modbus datasets. Layer 3 parses  the traffic into a shared, protocol -aware common 
feature repository, handling cleaning, labelling and the train/test split used by every detection module. 
Layer 4 contains four parallel detection modules - RP1 (DoS), RP2 (Reconnaissance, this component), 
RP3 (Injection) and RP4 (Replay) - each applying the methodology best matched to the behavioral 
signature of its target attack. Layer 5 fuses the per -module anomaly scores into a unified verdict and 
correlates alerts across modules. Layer 6 adds attack -type classification, severity ranking  and 
explainable-AI attribution, and Layer 7 presents real -time visualization, alerts and analysis reports 
through a unified incident dashboard. Data flows sequentially from Layer 1 through to Layer 7, with a 
feedback loop from the incident-analysis layer back to the detection modules to support periodic model 
retraining.

Individual Component within the Architecture 
Within this architecture, the reconnaissance component (RP2) sits in Layer 4 as one of four parallel 
detection modules. It consumes protocol-aware features from the shared Layer 3 repository - function-
code buckets, register/coil addresses, transaction IDs and read/write behavior - and constructs time -
windowed sequences from them. It trains and applies an LSTM model to classify each window as 
Normal or Reconnaissance/Scanning, and outputs a class, a confidence score and the contributing 
behavioral features to the Layer 5 decision-fusion layer, which combines this output with the other three 
modules' scores before Layer 6 adds explanation and severity ranking for the operator-facing dashboard 
in Layer 7. 
Overall Integration 
The four components integrate through the shared common feature repository (Layer 3) and a common 
internal data contract: each detection module reads a standardized, protocol-aware feature record keyed 
by session ID and timestamp, and publishes a standardi zed output record (module identifier, predicted 
class, confidence score and contributing features) to the decision -fusion layer (Layer 5). This avoids 
duplicated preprocessing, ensures all four modules share a consistent view of the traffic, and supports 
cross-module correlation - for example, a reconnaissance alert immediately followed by an injection 
alert on the same session can be correlated into a single multi -stage incident. The planned integration 
method is therefore a shared-repository, contract-based integration rather than direct module-to-module 
calls, which keeps each research component - including this one - independently developable and 
testable.

5. USER REQUIREMENTS 
The primary users of this component are Security Operations Centre (SOC) analysts and OT/ICS 
engineers who monitor Modbus -based networks. Because the group's ethical clearance restricts this 
project to traffic generated in an isolated testbed and public da tasets, with no human -subject data 
collected, requirements were not elicited through direct stakeholder interviews at proposal stage; instead 
they were derived from the literature on ICS/SOC alert handling [6], [7], [15], the gap identified in 
Section 1.3, and discussion with academic supervisors regarding realistic SOC operating constraints 
(alert volume, false -positive tolerance, and the need for explainable output). These findings were 
translated into the functional and non -functional requirements below,  which will be reviewed with 
supervisors and, where possible, domain practitioners as the project proceeds. 
Functional Requirements 
• FR1: The system shall classify each windowed Modbus communication sequence as Normal or 
Reconnaissance/Scanning. 
• FR2: The system shall output a confidence score with each classification. 
• FR3: The system shall identify the behavioral features that most contributed to a reconnaissance 
classification, to support the shared explainable-AI layer. 
• FR4: The system shall log detected reconnaissance events with a timestamp, source session 
identifier and confidence score to support forensic review. 
• FR5: The system shall publish its classification output to the shared decision -fusion layer in the 
agreed common data format. 
• FR6: The system shall support retraining on newly captured or relabeled sessions without requiring 
changes to the shared feature repository scheme. 
 
Non-Functional Requirements 
• NFR1 (Performance): The system shall classify a new sequence window within a detection-latency 
budget suitable for near-real-time SOC monitoring (target: on the order of seconds per window), 
measured under test load. 
• NFR2 (Reliability): The system shall achieve a false-positive rate below a defined threshold (target: 
under 5% on session-independent evaluation) to avoid alert fatigue. 
• NFR3 (Security): All development and testing traffic shall be captured and processed within the 
isolated testbed environment, with no access to live production ICS networks. 
• NFR4 (Scalability): The system shall be able to process traffic from multiple concurrent Modbus 
sessions without a proportional loss of detection accuracy. 
• NFR5 (Usability / Explainability): The system's output shall integrate into the shared incident 
dashboard using a consistent schema and  shall be interpretable enough for a SOC analyst to 
understand why an alert was raised without inspecting raw model internals.

• Target market and customer persona: OT/ICS -SCADA security teams, managed security service 
providers (MSSPs), and critical-infrastructure operators in energy and utilities, water treatment and 
smart manufacturing; the primary persona is a SOC analyst or OT security engineer responsible

• Value proposition: an affordable, protocol -aware, explainable reconnaissance detector that 
identifies scanning behavior missed by signature-based and volume-based tools, without requiring 
proprietary deep-packet-inspection hardware [15].

framework, priced per monitored device/session, or released with an open -source core and paid 
enterprise support and integration services.

appliances.

sessions, consistent with typical OT-security SaaS pricing. 
• Competitive advantage: temporal, protocol -aware modelling of reconnaissance behavior that 
generic signature-based competitors do not offer, combined with integration into a unified multi -
attack-class framework rather than a single-purpose point tool. 
• Intellectual property considerations: the novelty lies in the temporal feature-engineering approach 
and its integration into the shared framework; underlying open -source components (Python, 
TensorFlow/Keras, ICSSIM) remain under their existing open-source licenses, while the resulting
