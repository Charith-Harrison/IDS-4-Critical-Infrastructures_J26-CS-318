ABSTRACT  
 
Industrial Control Systems and SCADA environments frequently use Modbus/TCP because of 
its simplicity and long-standing industrial adoption. However, the protocol was not originally 
designed with strong authentication, confidentiality, or integrity protect ion. This creates a 
practical replay -attack risk: an adversary can capture a legitimate Modbus request and 
retransmit it later, while the replayed packet may still contain a valid function code, register 
address, value, and packet structure.  
 
The individual research problem is therefore not simply to identify malformed traffic, but to 
distinguish malicious re -use of valid transactions from legitimate repetitive industrial 
communication. Existing packet -content, static -signature, and generic flo w approaches are 
insufficient when the malicious packet is syntactically valid and only becomes suspicious when 
its relationship with earlier transactions is examined.  
 
The proposed contribution is a protocol -aware replay -detection component based on 
transaction-consistency analysis. Modbus/TCP packets will be reconstructed into transaction -
level records, and features including transaction identifiers, transaction -ID chan ges, 
request/response timing, duplicate behaviour, repeated payload patterns, function codes, 
register information, session context, and sequence consistency will be extracted. These 
structured features will be supplied to an XGBoost binary classifier that labels traffic as normal 
or replay.  
 
The component will be evaluated using labelled public or controlled testbed traffic, with 
emphasis on precision, recall, F1-score, false-positive rate, ROC-AUC, and detection latency. 
A key validation experiment will compare transaction -consistency features against 
conventional packet/flow features using a consistent classifier. The resulting replay score will 
be integrated into the wider project’s decision-fusion and incident-analysis layer. The intended 
outcome is a defensible, low -cost and explainable replay -detection mechanism for Modbus -
based industrial environments. 
 
Keywords: Modbus/TCP, Replay Attack Detection, Transaction Consistency, Machine 
learning, Industrial Control Systems

1. INTRODUCTION 
 
1.1 Background and Context 
Industrial control systems  (ICS) are the computer systems and machines that keep really 
important infrastructure running. This includes things most people don't think about every day, 
like power stations that make our electricity, water treatment plants that make our water safe to 
drink, pipelines that carry oil and gas, and factories that make the things we use. A lot of these 
systems talk to each other over a network using a communication method called Modbus, and 
specifically a version made for network use called Modbus/TCP 
. 
The problem with Modbus is that it's genuinely old. It was conceived in the 1970s, when no 
one in the industry was interested in hackers or cyber security because these industrial networks 
were almost always isolated from the outside world. This means that Modbus is not a password 
protected, encrypted, or has no built-in method of determining if a message originated from a 
trusted device or if it was altered during transmission. If the attacker succeeds in gaining access 
to the network, the machines cannot tell whether the command is a legitimate one from a trusted 
source or a bogus one issued by a malicious attacker. 
 
This used to be a smaller problem when these industrial networks were physically isolated from 
everything else. But that's not how most modern factories and utility companies work anymore. 
To make monitoring easier and to let engineers check on their systems remotely, more and more 
of these ICS networks are now connected to normal company networks and, in some cases, 
directly to the internet. This has made things much more dangerous, because it has massively 
increased the number of ways an attacker could actually reach these systems [4], [11]. 
 
Given this increased exposure to the outside world, there are now various types of attacks that 
attackers can make against Modbus-based systems. A Denial-of-Service attack is an attack that 
takes place when the attacker sends a massive amount of requests to exhaust the resource. They 
may enter the network without raising suspicion and determine what devices are in the network 
and what they are doing (reconnaissance). They can be able to send fake command that appears 
to be a real command, known as command and response injection. The attacker can also 
intercept an actual command that had previously been sent and repeat it later causing the same 
action to be repeated, kn own as a replay attack. The four attack types are separated into four 
different sub-groups of our group project, so that each group can catch the respective attack 
type correctly without trying to create a tool which would try to catch everything. 
 
Most of the security tools that are actually used in these environments today rely on either fixed, 
hand-written rules, or on looking at very general network measurements, for example, how big 
a message is, or how often messages are being sent, without pay ing any attention to what a 
Modbus message actually says or means [1], [6]. Because of this, these tools often fail to catch 
attacks that are slow, quiet, or that look almost exactly like a burst of normal, heavy traffic. At 
the same time, they tend to rai se way too many false alarms, which causes real operators to 
eventually start ignoring the alerts altogether [10], [15]. 
 
This research supports Sustainable Development Goal 9, Industry, Innovation and 
Infrastructure, because it works directly on making industrial systems more resistant to attacks 
that could damage physical infrastructure. It also supports Sustainable Develop ment Goal 7, 
Affordable and Clean Energy, since a lot of Modbus systems are found specifically inside 
energy and utility companies. Our project fits within the AIMS research group and the Cyber 
Security and Network Engineering research cluster.

1.2 Literature Review 
 
1.2.1 Existing Research Approaches 
 
Existing research on Modbus/TCP security can be grouped into several broad approaches: 
protocol protection, signature -based intrusion detection, protocol -aware modelling, replay -
specific detection, and machine -learning-based classification. Preventive work  proposed a 
secure Modbus design, while investigated the use of message authentication codes for 
protecting Modbus/TCP -based industrial automation systems [9]. These studies show that 
authentication and message integrity are important security requirements . However, they 
mainly focus on strengthening communication itself. In many existing industrial environments, 
changing the underlying protocol or replacing field devices may not be practical. Therefore,  
takes a complementary monitoring-based approach rather than modifying Modbus/TCP. 
 
Replay-focused research is particularly relevant to this study. Mo, Chabukswar and Sinopoli 
investigated integrity -attack detection in SCADA systems [1], while Naha et al. explored 
sequential replay-attack detection [2]. These studies collectively indicate  that replay attacks 
cannot always be identified from a single observation. Instead, timing, sequence, state, and 
relationships between events can provide stronger evidence. This directly supports th is 
component concept of analyzing  transactions across time rather than inspecting packets 
independently. 
 
In a Modbus-specific context, R. L. and P. Satyanarayana studied the detection and blocking 
of replay, false-command, and false-access injection attacks [11]. Deng et al. applied Support 
Vector Machines to Modbus/TCP intrusion detection [4], while Wang et al. and Goldenberg 
and Wool demonstrated the importance of Modbus -specific modelling for intrusion detection  
[13]. Gao and Morris further examined signature-based detection for Modbus industrial control 
systems [15]. These studies support the decision to use protocol -aware information instead of 
relying only on generic network-flow characteristics. 
 
Machine-learning research provides the implementation basis for this. Benka et al. investigated 
machine-learning-based detection of anomalies and intrusions in industrial control systems [8]. 
For this component, XGBoost is selected as the classifier because the proposed preprocessing 
stage converts communication behaviour into structured transaction-level features. XGBoost is 
suitable for this type of tabular data and can model interactions between several variables while 
also supporting feature -importance analysis [16]. The research contribution, however, is not 
the use of XGBoost alone; it is the use of transaction consistency features specifically designed 
for replay detection. 
 
1.2.2 Existing Products and Services 
 
The references supplied for RP4 mainly describe academic methods and security mechanisms 
rather than detailed commercial products. Therefore, the most defensible comparison is 
between existing categories of practical security solutions. 
 
One category is protocol -hardening solutions, where cryptographic mechanisms such as 
message authentication are added to Modbus communication [7]. These solutions can provide 
strong protection against message manipulation when both endpoints support the re quired 
changes. Their practical limitation is that existing industrial systems may include legacy PLCs, 
controllers, and software that cannot easily be modified.

A second category is signature-based intrusion detection. Gao and Morris showed how Modbus 
attacks can be identified using known signatures [15]. Such systems are useful when malicious 
behaviour has a recognizable pattern, but they are less reliable when a  replayed message is 
copied directly from legitimate communication. A third category is protocol -aware anomaly 
detection, where Modbus function codes, request -response behaviour , and communication 
patterns are modelled rather than treating traffic as generic TCP data [12].  
 
1.2.3 Existing System Limitations 
 
The main limitation identified from the literature is that replay attacks can remain valid at the 
packet-content level. A captured Modbus request may contain a legitimate function code, valid 
register address, valid value, and correct protocol structure. If that request is transmitted again 
later, a detector that examines only packet syntax or a known signature may not recognize it as 
malicious. Replay -oriented studies therefore support the need for temporal and sequential 
reasoning. 
 
Another limitation is the risk of over -relying on one indicator, particularly the transaction 
identifier. Transaction IDs are useful for matching Modbus requests and responses, but 
legitimate implementations may increment, reset, wrap, or reuse them differ ently. Treating a 
repeated transaction ID as sufficient evidence of replay could therefore produce false positives. 
 
For this reason, RP4 proposes a broader transaction-consistency representation. The model will 
combine transaction ID behaviour  with response timing, duplicate requests, register 
information, request-response matching, source and destination context, inter-arrival time, and 
session consistency. The central research gap is therefore the lack of sufficient evaluation of 
whether a co mbined set of transaction -level consistency features can reliably distinguish 
malicious replay traffic from legitimate repetitive Modbus/TCP communication. This 
component addresses this gap by engineering these features and evaluating them with an 
XGBoost classifier using labelled normal and replay traffic.

1.3 Research Gap 
 
The reviewed work establishes that Modbus security, replay attacks, protocol -aware intrusion 
detection, and machine -learning IDS have all been investigated. The remaining gap for this 
component is more specific. Many existing approaches still depend on packet content, static 
signatures, or generic traffic statistics. These methods can detect malformed, high -volume, or 
known attacks, but replayed Modbus transactions are more difficult because the retransmitted 
packet may still be completely valid [12]. The real evidence of malicious behaviour may 
therefore appear only when the transaction is compared with surrounding communication. 
 
This research addresses that limitation through transaction -consistency analysis. Instead of 
treating a repeated packet as automatically malicious, the proposed method evaluates 
transaction identifiers, response timing, duplicate behaviour, request -response relationships, 
session consistency, and transaction sequence. Replay-focused studies support the importance 
of temporal and sequential evidence [10], while Modbus-specific research highlights the value 
of protocol-aware modelling [13]. 
 
The novelty of RP4 lies in representing replay behaviour through relationships across 
transactions rather than relying on one packet or one feature. This is particularly important 
because legitimate Modbus communication often contains repeated polling, ret ries, and stable 
register reads [12]. The proposed approach therefore aims to detect suspicious repetition while 
maintaining a low false-positive rate.

2. OBJECTIVE 
 
2.1 Main Objective 
 
To develop and evaluate a protocol -aware machine -learning component that distinguishes 
replayed Modbus/TCP transactions from legitimate communication by analyzing transaction  
consistency across transaction identifiers, response timing, duplicate requests, request/ response 
relationships, sequence behaviour, and session context, and to integrate the resulting detection 
output with the overall project’s decision-fusion and incident-analysis layer. 
 
2.2 Specific Objectives 
 
1. To capture, identify, and prepare labelled normal and replay-attack Modbus/TCP traffic 
using suitable public industrial datasets and an isolated controlled Modbus testbed. 
 
2. To reconstruct packet captures into transaction -level records and engineer protocol -
aware features covering MBAP fields, Modbus commands, timing, duplication, 
sequence, request/response consistency, session context, and expected polling 
behaviour. 
 
3. To develop an XGBoost -based supervised binary classifier that labels each analyzed 
transaction or transaction window as Normal or Replay Attack. 
 
4. To quantify the contribution of transaction -consistency features by comparing the 
proposed feature representation with a baseline using conventional packet/flow features 
under a controlled and leakage-resistant evaluation procedure. 
 
5. To evaluate detection quality using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 -score, false-positive 
rate, confusion-matrix analysis, and detection latency, with particular attention to recall, 
F1-score, false-positive rate, and latency, ROC-AUC. 
 
6. To expose the replay -detection result, confidence score, relevant evidence, and attack 
metadata in a format that can be consumed by the common decision-fusion and incident-
analysis layer of the overall Modbus attack-detection framework.

3. METHODOLOGY 
 
3.1 Research Methodology and Design 
 
This research will use a design -oriented experimental methodology. The work has two 
inseparable outputs: a technical artefact and empirical evidence about that artefact. The 
technical artefact is the RP4 replay -detection module. The empirical contribution is the 
evaluation of whether transaction-consistency features improve the ability to distinguish replay 
attacks from legitimate repetitive Modbus/TCP behaviour. A purely descriptive study would 
not be sufficient because the central claim must be tested through controlled data, trained 
models, baseline comparisons, and measured outcomes. 
 
The methodology follows a claim -evidence-decision logic. The claim is that replay detection 
requires cross -transaction consistency information. The evidence will come from labelled 
normal and replay traffic, feature -level analysis, baseline experiments, ablation tests, and 
performance metrics. The decision will be whether the proposed transaction -consistency 
representation is justified for integration into the final multi -attack framework. This structure 
directly matches the proposal template’s expectation that te chnical choices be evidence-based 
rather than merely listed. 
 
The main experimental unit will be a Modbus transaction or a short transaction context derived 
from captured traffic. Raw PCAP traffic will not be given directly to XGBoost. It will first be 
parsed into requests and responses, associated with TCP sessions, ordered by timestamp, and 
converted into structured records. Features will then describe both the current transaction and 
its relationship to previous relevant transactions. This design ensures that the model is learning 
replay-related consistency patterns rather than memorizing arbitrary raw packet bytes.

3.2 Data / Participants / Experimental Inputs 
 
This study requires labelled Modbus/TCP traffic containing both legitimate communication and 
replay-attack behaviour. This type of data directly supports the RP4 research question, which 
investigates whether replayed transactions can be separated from legitimate repetitive traffic 
using transaction -level consistency rather than packet content alone. The main evidence 
required includes transaction identifiers, response timing, duplicate requests, request -response 
relationships, register information, and session consistency.  
 
Two experimental data sources will be used. The primary public dataset selected for the study 
is CIC Modbus 2023 , which will be used as the main benchmark dataset for training and 
evaluating the machine -learning model. Before model development, the available records, 
labels, protocol fields, and class distribution will be inspected to determine which data can 
directly support the replay-detection task. Where replay-specific transaction information is not 
sufficiently represented, the public dataset will be complemented with traffic generated through 
a controlled Modbus/TCP testbed . This approach is consistent with the RP4 methodology, 
which proposes using normal traffic, controlled replay traffic, suitable public ICS/SCADA data, 
and testbed-generated communication.  
 
The testbed will consist of a Modbus master and PLC/slave environment. Normal 
communication will first be captured to represent legitimate polling and repeated industrial 
operations. Replay scenarios will then be generated by capturing valid Modbus transactions and 
retransmitting them under controlled conditions. Wireshark/Tshark, Scapy, and PyModbus will 
support packet capture, manipulation, and Modbus simulation, with traffic stored in PCAP and 
processed CSV formats.  
 
The experimental sample will consist of reconstructed Modbus transaction records, labelled as 
Normal (0) or Replay Attack (1) . No human participants are involved because the research 
analyzes network communication rather than personal data. During preprocessing, packets will 
be parsed and paired into request -response transactions. Features such as transaction ID 
behaviour, inter -arrival time, response latency, duplicate frequency, function code, register 
information, session context, and transaction sequence will then be extracted.  
Finally, the dataset will be cleaned, checked for missing or invalid records, encoded where 
required, and separated into training and testing data while avoiding data leakage. Legitimate 
repeated transactions will intentionally be retained because distingu ishing normal repetition 
from malicious replay is central to the contribution of RP4. 
 
3.3 Baseline and Benchmark Plan 
 
The strongest self -evaluation design is to compare the proposed transaction -consistency 
representation against a simpler representation while keeping the classifier a s constant as 
possible. Baseline A will therefore use XGBoost trained on conventional packet/flow or 
current-packet features available from the same traffic. Proposed Model B will use XGBoost 
with the transaction -consistency feature set. If Model B produce s higher replay recall or F1 -
score and a lower false -positive rate on unseen sessions, the evidence directly supports the 
proposed novelty. 
 
A second baseline will be a transparent rule-based detector using obvious replay indicators such 
as duplicate payload or repeated transaction identifier within a time window. This baseline is

intentionally simple. Its role is to demonstrate why a single rule is insufficient in normal 
repetitive industrial traffic. A third optional baseline may use an SVM or another conventional 
supervised classifier if the final schedule permits. However, addin g many models is less 
important than evaluating the research claim cleanly. 
 
The comparison will use the same train/test scenarios, labels, and metric definitions. 
Hyperparameter tuning will be performed independently but only on training/validation data. 
The final test set will remain untouched until the experiment design is fixed. Where data from 
both public sources and the testbed are available, cross-source evaluation will be attempted, for 
example training on one set and testing on a separate capture source. Even if performance 
decreases, such a result is useful because it reveals the generalization limits of the method. 
 
3.4 Evaluation Metrics  
 
Accuracy will report the proportion of all evaluated events classified correctly, but it will not 
be treated as the main success measure because class imbalance can make accuracy misleading. 
Precision will measure how many transactions predicted as replay are act ually replay. Recall 
will measure how many true replay events are detected. F1 -score will combine precision and 
recall through their harmonic mean. False -positive rate will measure the proportion of 
legitimate events incorrectly labelled as replay. ROC-AUC will summarize the model’s ability 
to discriminate across thresholds. 
 
No unsupported numerical performance target such as “99% accuracy” will be promised before 
experiments. Instead, the proposal defines comparative success criteria. The proposed model 
should improve replay recall and F1 -score over the packet/flow baseline, should reduce false 
positives relative to a simple duplicate/TID rule, and should operate with latency appropriate 
for near-real-time incident analysis. These are defensible criteria because they measure whether 
the proposed transaction representation adds value.

3.5 Validation Strategy 
 
Validation will occur at four levels. First, data validation will confirm that labelled replay events 
were actually generated as intended and that normal captures contain realistic repetitive 
behaviour. Packet-level inspection with Wireshark or Tshark and scripted checks will be used 
to confirm the ground truth. Second, model validation will use separated training/validation/test 
data with scenario or session -aware splitting. Confusion matrices and threshold analysis will 
reveal whether errors concentrate in particular traffic patterns. 
 
Third, feature valid ation will test the novelty claim. Ablatio n experiments will remove one 
feature group at a time  for example transaction, timing, duplication, or session features  and 
observe the effect on F1, recall and FPR. Feature importance and, if required, SHAP 
explanations will identify which variables contribute to the XGBoost decisions. This does not 
by itself prove causality, but it provides interpretable evidence about the learned detector. 
 
Fourth, integration validation will verify that can produce a consiste nt output record for the 
common framework. The record should contain timestamp, session or flow key, replay label, 
probability/confidence, relevant evidence features, and processing latency. The decision-fusion 
layer should be able to consume the output wi thout needing access to it’s internal model. This 
preserves modularity and makes the component independently testable. 
 
Reliability tests will include repeated normal scenarios, variations in polling interval, different 
replay delays, multiple function cod es where feasible, session restarts, and transaction -ID 
wrap/reuse conditions if they can be generated safely. The purpose is not to make the testbed 
artificially complex, but to challenge the shortcuts that would otherwise make replay detection 
appear easier than it is. 
 
3.6 Development Approach 
 
An iterative prototyping approach will be used. The first iteration will establish packet capture, 
Modbus parsing, and transaction reconstruction. The second will create the baseline feature 
table and simple duplica te/TID detector. The third will implement the full transaction -
consistency feature set and XGBoost model. The fourth will focus on feature ablation, latency 
measurement, and false-positive analysis. The final iteration will expose the model through an 
integration interface and connect it to the common decision-fusion layer. 
 
This approach is preferred to a single waterfall implementation because the research contains 
uncertainty. Dataset fields may differ, replay timing may need adjustment, and some features  
may prove unreliable after inspection. Iterative development allows each assumption to be 
tested before the next layer is built. Version -controlled code, experiment configuration files, 
fixed random seeds where applicable, and saved model artifacts will support reproducibility.

4. HIGH-LEVEL SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 
 
 
Figure 2: High-level system architecture 
The high -level architecture of the proposed protocol -aware Modbus/TCP attack detection 
framework. The architecture follows a structured processing flow beginning with the 
Modbus/TCP testbed, where normal and attack traffic are generated between the SCADA/HMI 
master and PLC/RTU devices. Captured traffic is then passed to the data acquisition layer, 
where packet captures and labelled traffic are prepared for further processing. The next stage 
performs Modbus/TCP parsing, transaction reconstruction, protocol -aware feature extraction, 
data cleaning, and normalization. A shared feature repository is used so that all research 
components work with a consistent representation of the captured traffic. 
 
After preprocessing, the extracted information is supplied to the parallel attack -detection 
modules. Each research component applies an attack -specific detection strategy rather than 
relying on one generic model for every Modbus attack. The outputs from th ese modules are 
forwarded to the decision -fusion layer, where individual results are combined to produce the 
final attack classification and alert information. The resulting detection information is then 
displayed through the dashboard and reporting layer for monitoring and analysis. 
 
The individual contribution of this research is Replay Attack Detection using Transaction 
Consistency Analysis . Replay attacks are challenging because an attacker may retransmit a 
previously legitimate Modbus message, meaning that the packet itself can still appear valid. 
Therefore, it does not depend only on packet content. First, Modbus request -response 
transactions are reconstructed from the captured traffic. Transaction -consistency features are 
then extracted, including transaction ID behaviour, response timing, duplicate requests, register 
information, and session consistency. These structured features are provided to an XGBoost 
classifier, which learns patterns that distinguish legitimate repeated communication from 
malicious replay behaviour. The model produces a normal/replay classification together with a 
confidence score, and this result is forwarded to the common decision-fusion layer. This design 
keeps RP4 clearly separated as an individual research contribution while allowing it to operate 
as part of the complete Modbus attack-detection framework.

4.1 Overall Integration 
 
The overall project contains separate attack-specific research components that share a common 
protocol-aware pipeline. This component does not need to know the internal algorithms of the 
DoS, reconnaissance, or command/response injection modules. Its dependency is the common 
parsed traffic representation and the agreed output contract to the fusion layer. This is an 
important architectural boundary: the replay component can be developed, tested, and scored 
independently while still participating in the final multi-attack system. 
 
The planned integration method is a structured record or service interface. The shared parser 
provides timestamped Modbus transactions and session metadata. This component returns 
fields such as attack_type = replay, predicted_label, confidence, event_time , session_id, 
evidence summary, and model latency. The fusion layer can then combine the component result 
with other module outputs and deduplicate correlated alerts. This design minimizes coupling 
and allows the replay model to be retrained without changing the rest of the system.

6. COMMERCIALIZATION PLAN 
  
6.1 Target market, customer persona, and key stakeholders 
 
The proposed Protocol -Aware Machine Learning Framework for Intelligent Modbus Attack 
Detection is intended for organizations that depend on Modbus/TCP -based industrial control 
systems but may not have the budget or specialist staff required for large commercial OT -
security platforms. Suitable customers include small and medium manufacturing plants, water 
and wastewater facilities, energy and utility operators, process industries, and other 
organizations operating SCADA, PLC and RTU environments. These sectors are relevant 
because Modbus remains important in industrial communication, while attacks against control 
devices can cause operational disruption, equipment problems and safety concerns.  
 
The main user persona is an OT/SCADA engineer, automation technician or cybersecurity 
engineer responsible for maintaining reliable industrial operations. Plant managers and asset 
owners are also important stakeholders because they are concerned with downt ime, security 
expenditure and operational continuity. System integrators may support deployment and 
configuration, while the development team would be responsible for maintaining the detection 
models, protocol parser and software releases. 
 
6.2 Value proposition 
 
The proposed framework provides an affordable, non -intrusive, software -based intrusion 
detection system specially developed for Modbus/TCP industrial networks. Rather than 
inspecting individual packets with fragile, manually crafted rules, the system exami nes 
protocol-aware semantics for all four of the primary attack vectors in ICS: Denial -of-Service, 
Reconnaissance, Command/Response Injection, and Replay. It is passive technology that works 
on standard off-the-shelf industrial PCs or edge servers, with no need of specialized hardware 
or changes to existing PLCs. Additionally, the multi -module detection and Layer 5 decision 
fusion, along with Layer 6 explainability, provide operators with a single consolidated alert and 
explanations of the individual transactions, not multiple conflicting alerts. 
 
6.3 Revenue model 
 
A flexible hybrid revenue model is established to accommodate both operating expenditure 
(OpEx) and capital expenditure (CapEx) purchasing preferences common in industrial 
organizations: 
• Tier 1: Annual / Multi-Year Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) Subscription (OpEx) 
o Provides ongoing access to the complete four-module detection engine, unified 
fusion dashboard, continuous background threat modeling, and routine software 
maintenance.  
o Billed annually or as a 3 -year enterprise license per monitored SCADA subnet 
or gateway. 
• Tier 2: Perpetual License with Annual Maintenance Contract (CapEx) 
o A one-time upfront software purchase intended for air -gapped or high-security 
facilities that do not permit recurring cloud billing. 
o Coupled with an optional Annual Maintenance Contract (AMC)  (priced at 
approximately 18-20% of the initial license cost per year) covering technical 
support, bug fixes, periodic rule updates, and scheduled model recalibration.

6.7 Competitive advantage 
 
The component’s competitive advantage is its specific focus on protocol -valid replay 
behaviour. Signature -based Modbus IDS remains useful for known attacks [15], and 
cryptographic protection is stronger when it can be deployed [7], [9]. The proposed detect or 
occupies a different space: it can be added as a monitoring layer and can learn patterns of 
transaction inconsistency without requiring every field device to support a modified security 
protocol. Its integration with a wider multi -attack framework also increases value because a 
customer would receive a common incident view rather than four disconnected research tools. 
 
The strongest defensible differentiator is not the use of XGBoost, since XGBoost is a known 
algorithm [16]. The potential intellectual property lies in the engineered replay -consistency 
pipeline, the feature definitions, the transaction -history logic, the model calibration and 
deployment process, and the way RP4 evidence is fused with other Modbus attack signals. 
Software copyright can protect the implementation. Any claim of patentability would require 
professional novelty assessment and should not be assumed in this proposal. 
 
6.8 Intellectual property considerations 
 
All algorithms, protocol parsing code, trained machine learning model weights, fusion logic, 
and documentation developed across the project belong to the student research team and the 
university under SLIIT's standard research and intellectual property reg ulations. Third-party 
academic datasets utilized for training and benchmarking (such as the CIC Modbus 2023 dataset 
and the MSU/ORNL Gas Pipeline dataset) are restricted strictly to academic benchmarking in 
accordance with their respective non -commercial open data  licenses [2], [19]. Commercial 
releases will utilize proprietary baseline profiles and synthetic testbed traffic generated 
exclusively within our controlled laboratory environment.
